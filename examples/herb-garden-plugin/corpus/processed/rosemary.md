## Watering

Rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) is a Mediterranean herb that tolerates dry spells far better than it tolerates soggy soil, so let the top two inches of soil dry out before watering Rosemary again. Overwatering is the most common way to lose a container-grown Rosemary, since its roots rot quickly in soil that stays wet. In summer heat, Rosemary in a small pot may need water once or twice a week, while in cooler months once every ten to fourteen days is often enough. Always water Rosemary deeply when you do water, letting excess drain fully out of the pot rather than leaving a saucer of standing water underneath. If the lower stems of Rosemary feel soft rather than woody, ease back on watering immediately.

## Light

Rosemary needs at least six hours of direct sun a day and grows best in a hot, bright spot such as a south-facing window or an open patio. Indoor Rosemary kept in low light quickly grows thin and sparse, with soft new growth prone to disease. A supplemental grow light held close to the plant for twelve hours a day can keep indoor Rosemary full when a bright enough window is not available. Outdoors, Rosemary tolerates intense summer sun and reflected heat from a wall or patio better than most herbs. Moving Rosemary gradually into brighter light over one to two weeks helps the plant acclimate without scorching its needle-like leaves.

## Toxicity

The cited reference does not list Rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) as toxic to cats or dogs and identifies it as a common culinary and landscaping herb. Even so, the cited reference notes that a pet eating a large quantity of any plant material, including Rosemary, can experience mild stomach upset such as vomiting or diarrhea. If a cat or dog eats an unusually large amount of Rosemary and shows ongoing symptoms, contact a veterinarian or a poison-control line for guidance rather than waiting it out. The cited reference addresses only the toxicity question and does not evaluate the woody stems as a separate choking hazard.

## Soil and repotting

Rosemary needs a fast-draining mix, so blend regular potting soil with extra perlite or coarse sand the way you would for a cactus or succulent. Plant Rosemary in a container with generous drainage holes, since standing water at the roots is the fastest way to lose the plant. Repot Rosemary every two to three years, or sooner if roots begin circling densely at the bottom of the pot. Choose a new pot for Rosemary only slightly larger than the old one, because an oversized container holds more moisture than the roots of Rosemary can use. After repotting, wait several days before watering Rosemary so any disturbed roots have time to settle.

## Common problems

Browning, dropping needles on Rosemary usually point to overwatering and the early stages of root rot rather than underwatering, despite Rosemary's reputation for toughness. A white, powdery coating on Rosemary leaves signals powdery mildew, which is encouraged by poor air circulation and can be reduced by spacing plants and watering at the soil line. Soft, blackened stems near the base of Rosemary are a sign of advanced root rot, usually requiring the plant to be repotted into dry, fast-draining soil or discarded if the rot has spread far. Leggy, woody growth with few new leaves on an older Rosemary responds well to a hard prune in spring, which encourages fresh, tender growth. Rosemary that smells musty rather than aromatic when brushed often has roots sitting in soil that has stayed wet for too long.
