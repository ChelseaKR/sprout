## Watering

Basil (Ocimum basilicum) wilts quickly when the soil dries out, so keep the top inch of soil consistently moist rather than letting Basil dry out completely between waterings. Water Basil at the base of the plant so the leaves stay dry, since wet foliage left overnight invites fungal spots on Basil. In a warm, sunny spot Basil in a container often needs water every one to two days during summer, while indoor Basil in cooler rooms can go three to four days. Always let excess water drain freely, because Basil planted in a pot without drainage holes is prone to root rot. Check the soil with a finger before watering; if the top inch of soil still feels damp, wait another day before watering Basil again.

## Light

Basil needs at least six hours of direct sun each day to grow full and bushy, so a south-facing windowsill or an open patio spot suits Basil best. Indoor Basil grown with fewer than six hours of light tends to grow tall and leggy with widely spaced leaves. If natural light is limited, a full-spectrum grow light held close to the plant for twelve to fourteen hours a day can substitute for direct sun. Rotate a windowsill pot of Basil every few days so all sides receive even light and the plant does not lean permanently toward the window. Basil grown in too little light also tends to produce smaller, less fragrant leaves than Basil grown in full sun.

## Toxicity

The cited reference does not list Basil (Ocimum basilicum) as toxic to cats or dogs and identifies it as a common culinary herb grown in many kitchen gardens. Even so, the cited reference notes that a pet eating a large quantity of any plant material, including Basil, can experience mild stomach upset such as vomiting or diarrhea. If a cat or dog eats an unusually large amount of Basil and shows ongoing symptoms, contact a veterinarian or a poison-control line for guidance rather than waiting it out. The cited reference addresses only the toxicity question and does not evaluate choking risk from stems or other physical hazards.

## Soil and repotting

Basil grows best in a rich, well-draining potting mix amended with compost, since Basil planted in heavy, waterlogged soil is prone to root rot. Choose a container with drainage holes for Basil and size it up as the plant grows, moving Basil to a pot roughly two inches wider once roots begin circling the base. Repot Basil in early spring before the growing season accelerates, disturbing the roots as little as possible during the move. After repotting, water Basil thoroughly once and then let the topsoil begin to dry before watering again so the roots can recover.

## Common problems

Yellowing lower leaves on Basil usually point to overwatering or a pot without adequate drainage, so check the roots for sogginess before treating with fertilizer. Dark, water-soaked spots on Basil leaves typically indicate a fungal or bacterial leaf spot encouraged by wet foliage, so switch to watering at the soil line and improve airflow around the plant. Basil that bolts and sends up flower stalks in hot weather will taste more bitter, so pinch off flower buds promptly to keep the leaves productive. Leggy, sparse growth on Basil usually means insufficient light, and moving the plant to a brighter spot along with regular pinching of the growing tips encourages a fuller shape. Wilting Basil that recovers quickly after watering points to underwatering, while wilting that persists after watering can signal root rot from consistently soggy soil.
