## Watering

Mint (Mentha spicata) prefers consistently moist soil and does not tolerate drying out the way many other herbs do, so check Mint every day in hot weather. Water Mint whenever the top half inch of soil feels dry to the touch, and expect that a container of Mint may need water daily during summer heat. Because Mint's shallow, spreading roots sit near the surface, the pot should never sit fully dry for more than a day or the leaf edges of Mint will scorch and crisp. Always use a pot with drainage holes for Mint, since standing water at the base still causes root rot even though Mint likes damp soil. A saucer of water under the pot can help humidity-loving Mint in dry indoor air, as long as the pot itself is not sitting directly in standing water.

## Light

Mint grows well in bright, indirect light and tolerates partial shade better than most culinary herbs, making it a forgiving choice for an east-facing window. Four to six hours of morning sun suits Mint well, while intense afternoon sun in a hot climate can scorch its leaves. Indoors, Mint kept near a bright window without direct midday sun typically produces the fullest, most tender growth. If grown outdoors, partial shade during the hottest part of the day helps prevent Mint from wilting in summer heat. Mint grown in deep shade will still survive but produces thinner, less aromatic leaves than Mint grown in brighter conditions.

## Soil and repotting

Mint spreads aggressively by runners, so most growers keep Mint confined to its own container even when planted in a larger garden bed. Use a well-draining potting mix for Mint, and choose a wide, shallow container that gives the spreading roots of Mint room to fill in. Repot or divide Mint every one to two years once the roots become congested, since an overcrowded pot slows new growth. When repotting, trim back the runners of Mint to keep the plant compact and encourage fresh, tender leaves. After repotting, water Mint well and keep the soil evenly moist while the roots settle into the new container.

## Common problems

Powdery white patches on Mint leaves usually indicate powdery mildew, encouraged by poor air circulation and overhead watering, so space plants out and water at the soil line instead. Rust-colored spots on the undersides of Mint leaves point to mint rust, a fungal disease that spreads quickly through dense plantings and is best managed by removing affected stems. Wilting Mint that recovers soon after watering signals underwatering, which is common because Mint dries out faster than most herbs. Leggy, sparse Mint with long bare stems benefits from hard pinching back, which encourages the plant to branch out and produces denser new growth. Yellowing leaves low on the plant, combined with a musty smell from the soil, usually point to waterlogged roots even in a moisture-loving plant like Mint.
